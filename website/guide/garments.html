Garments: sewing patterns at real size

In short:

  • Garments are flat pattern panels at real size in millimetres, sewn edge to edge.
  • Templates starts a garment from your size chart; Print writes the pages.
  • A sewn pattern drapes on a body and becomes a Part you can sculpt.

A garment in SculptForge is made the way clothes are made: flat pattern panels at their real size in millimetres, sewn edge to edge. The same pattern prints on your home printer to cut real fabric, and drapes on your model (Drape it on a body).

Open Tool›Garments (the Pattern window shows the panels flat): Templates starts a garment at your sizes, Print writes the pages. Everything here is also a garment_* command for Scripting and the AI assistant ("make me trousers for a 1000 mm hip").

Your size chart#

Templates are drawn from a size chart saved in the project: type your measurements (or your model's) once and every template uses them. In Templates›Size chart (mm), type a value and press Enter (hover a name to see how to measure it); Default sizes puts them all back. From a script, garment_size lists the chart and takes any measurement in millimetres:

garment_size chest_mm=1020 waist_mm=860 hip_mm=1040 inseam_mm=800

Templates#

Pick one in Templates (or garment_new template=<name>): it replaces the scene's pattern with that garment at your sizes. Tick Add beside the pattern (add=true) to put it next to what is there, so one pattern can hold a whole outfit. Measurements given here go into the chart too, and length_mm sets the template's main length for this garment only.

TemplatePanelslength_mm is
tshirtfront, back, two short sleevesshoulder to hem (700)
trousersfront and back of each legthe inseam (shorts: 200 or so)
skirtstraight front and back, waistbandwaist to hem (waist to knee)
skirt_alineflared front and back, waistbandwaist to hem

Every template comes sewn the way that garment is put together, with notches where pieces meet and the seam lengths matched (garment_info lists every seam with both lengths). The hoodie's pocket is the one loose piece: topstitch it onto the front where you want it. The proportions (how much ease, how deep the armhole, how the crotch curve is shaped) are SculptForge's own, chosen to fit an average body without darts; they are a starting point to adjust, not a tailored block.

garment_size hip_mm=1050 inseam_mm=780
garment_new template=hoodie
garment_new template=trousers add=true
garment_info

Drape it on a body#

A sewn pattern drapes on any body in the scene and becomes a Part you can sculpt, paint and export. You put each panel where it goes; SculptForge sews the seams and lets the cloth settle. The Full Body starter mesh (File›New from starter) is a good first body.

1. Place the panels#

Start a garment (Templates›T-shirt), then under Drape on the body press Place panels. The list below the button shows every panel; the one waiting for a click is highlighted and the text under the button names it.

Click the body once for each panel, from the view that faces that panel. The panel appears a little in front of the click, facing you, its top up the screen. The panel's widest line goes at the click: a shirt's chest line (just under the armholes), a sleeve's line under its cap, trousers' hip line. So place each panel slightly in front of the body at its widest point:

PanelWhere to clickFrom
Frontthe front of the chestthe front
Backthe back, at chest heightbehind (turn the view round)
Sleevethe top of the upper arm, just below the shoulderoutside and a little above, so the arm runs up the screen toward the shoulder
Trouser legthe side of the hipthe side

After each click the list ticks the panel off and asks for the next one; when every panel is placed, the button lets go. Click a panel in the list to place it again. The placed panels show on the body as a preview (seams still open), and they are saved with the pattern.

2. Adjust a panel#

The last panel placed (or the one picked in the list) has three sliders:

  • Turn spins it in the view's plane: a sleeve's top toward the shoulder when the arm slants.
  • Wrap curls it back round the body: 0 is flat, 360 a closed tube. A panel sewn to itself (a sleeve, a trouser leg) starts as a nearly closed tube with its own seam on the far side from your click, so the arm or leg sits inside it.
  • Gap moves it out from the click (30 mm to start). Wherever the preview disappears into the body, raise Gap until it clears: the back of a T-shirt usually needs about 70 mm so its hem clears the hips.

3. Drape#

Drape sews every seam shut and lets the garment settle on the visible Parts, live (the body and anything else shown; hide Parts it shouldn't touch). It sews first with gravity off, then lets the cloth fall and stops on its own once it is still.

Pause holds it, Stop keeps it as it is now, Reset goes back to the placed panels. When it stops, the view shows the garment as Convert will make it, and the status line says how many frames it took and the widest seam gap (a few millimetres is normal: Convert closes it).

4. Convert to Part#

Convert to Part keeps it as the Part Garment: seams welded, the pattern's thickness (2 mm unless set), UVs from the flat pattern (each panel where it lies on the sheet, so a texture painted on the pattern lines up), one face group per panel. It is one undo step, and the body stays the active Part. To drape another garment, start or load its pattern and place it the same way.

Size and units#

The garment is at the size of its pattern, the size chart's measurements, never stretched to the body: if it is too loose or too tight, change the chart (or the model) and start the template again. A SculptForge unit is a metre; a body modelled bigger is read by its size: over 20 units counts as centimetres (the starter meshes are), over 600 as millimetres.

When something looks wrong#

  • The body shows through the cloth: the panel started inside the body. Raise its Gap and drape again.
  • A sleeve or leg slides off: look at the limb from outside so it runs up the screen, click its top, and check that the preview tube goes round it.
  • Cloth tucked behind the chin or a beard: cloth dropped from above settles behind overhangs. That is the physics, not a bug: place the panel in front of the body instead of above it.
  • A seam gap over 10 mm in the status line: a panel was placed far from where it is sewn. Place it closer to its neighbours and drape again.

From a script#

garment_place puts a panel on the body (point on the body, facing toward the viewer, up the panel's top, optional turn, wrap, gap_mm; clear=true takes it off) and garment_drape drapes and converts in one step (frames, fabric):

new_starter_mesh name=full-body
garment_new template=tshirt
garment_place panel=Front point=0,116,5 facing=0,0,1
garment_place panel=Back point=0,116,-15 facing=0,0,-1 gap_mm=70
garment_place panel="Left sleeve" point=27,133,-9.5 facing=0.88,0.47,0 up=-0.47,0.88,0
garment_place panel="Right sleeve" point=-27,133,-9.5 facing=-0.88,0.47,0 up=0.47,0.88,0
garment_drape

Print it at home#

Print shows how many pages the pattern takes on A4 or Letter (portrait or Landscape); Save PDF... writes them all in one PDF, Save SVG pages... one SVG per page. From a script: garment_export path=trousers.pdf paper=letter landscape=true.

  • Print at 100% ("actual size", never "fit to page"), then measure the 50 mm square on page A1.
  • Each page has a name: rows A, B, C down, columns 1, 2, 3 across. Pages with nothing on them are left out.
  • Trim each page on its line at the left and the top, lay it over its neighbours' lines and match the black triangles (each page prints half of each; the neighbour's name sits next to it). Tape, then cut.
  • A panel that is another's mirror image (the other leg, the other sleeve) prints once, named for example "Left front: cut 2, 1 reversed (Right front)": fold the fabric right sides together and cut through both layers.
  • Solid lines are the sewing lines, dashed lines the cutting lines (seam allowance: 10 mm, 5 mm on gloves). Ticks across the allowance are notches; the arrow is the grain line, kept parallel to the fabric's selvedge.

For a print shop or a plotter, Export SVG writes the whole pattern as one real-size sheet; Export DXF writes the outlines for a cutting machine.
